Florida cottage food rules, in plain English

A starting point for cooks — not legal advice. Updated August 2026.

Florida lets people sell certain homemade foods without a license, permit, or kitchen inspection. That's the "cottage food" law (Florida Statutes §500.80). It's genuinely generous — but it covers a specific list of foods, and a lot of classic meal prep isn't on it. Here's the honest picture.

What cottage food covers

Foods that don't need refrigeration to stay safe ("not potentially hazardous"). For example:

  • Breads, rolls, biscuits, cakes, pastries, and cookies (no cream or custard fillings)
  • Jams, jellies, and preserves
  • Granola, trail mix, dry soup mixes, and spice blends
  • Popcorn, nut butters, roasted nuts
  • Dried pasta and dried herbs

Cottage food sales must be direct to the consumer (which curbside pickup is), and there's an annual gross sales cap — currently $250,000.

What it doesn't cover

This is the part that matters for meal prep. Anything that needs refrigeration to stay safe is not cottage food: cooked meats and proteins, rice and cooked vegetables, soups and stews, casseroles, dairy-based dishes, cut melons and leafy-green salads. A classic burrito-bowl or lasagna drop falls in this category.

Selling those foods legally in Florida means operating as a licensed food establishment — typically by renting time in a commissary or shared commercial kitchen and getting a food permit from the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services (FDACS) or, for some operations, the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR).

Practical paths for a meal-prep cook

  1. Stay in cottage-food territory. Build drops around shelf-stable items: baked goods, granola, breads, jams, spice kits, dry soup mixes. No permit needed.
  2. Use a shared commercial kitchen. Many areas have commissary kitchens that rent by the hour. Cook there, get the FDACS food permit, and you can sell refrigerated meals legitimately.
  3. Cook for your circle. Sharing food with friends and neighbors without selling it isn't regulated — some cooks start there while they sort out a permit.

Labeling, if you sell cottage foods

Florida requires cottage food products to be labeled with the product name, your name and address, the ingredients, allergen information, net weight, and this exact statement:

"Made in a cottage food operation that is not subject to Florida's food safety regulations."

Masking tape and a marker technically count — but a printed label looks better on your drop.

Allergens, always

Whatever you sell, tell buyers about the big allergens in your food: milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame. The app's meal description and tags are the place; buyers' order notes will tell you about their allergies too.

Where to verify

  • Florida's cottage food statute: F.S. §500.80
  • FDACS cottage foods guidance: fdacs.gov — including how to get a food permit if you outgrow cottage food

Curbside Kitchens connects neighbors; it doesn't prepare food, process payments, or verify compliance. Selling food is your business — literally — so when in doubt, ask FDACS or a local attorney.
